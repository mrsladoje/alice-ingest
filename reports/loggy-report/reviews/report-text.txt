                                                    Abstract
         The ALICE Event Processing Nodes write logs that mostly never reach the tool people read with.
     loggy keeps most logs on the worker that wrote them and still searches every worker as one. One
     collector per worker reads five log families, stamps each line with a template and routes it by severity.
     Info, debug and trace lines stay in one local copy. Every other severity goes to three storage machines.
     Every worker pushes its health up. Nothing polls it. Threshold rules, Random Cut Forest detectors and
     trend rules turn numbers into episodes. It runs on five staging machines, and one farm deployment
     passed every check. In the busiest hour of six months of archive the median worker carried 23 records a
     second, and the busiest worker-second was 78. The collector costs about a quarter of one core at 20,000
     records a second. The whole stack on one worker sustains about 42,000 a second. A flush interval of
     1 second instead of 5 cut the collector’s cost by 24.5 % and its peak memory by 28 %. The templating
     pipeline is 31 to 57 % cheaper per family and byte-identical on 8,961,245 lines. Dense retrieval with
     reranking reaches 0.685 nDCG@10 against 0.634. The main limit: every cost figure comes from a laptop,
     so only shapes, knees and rankings transfer to the farm.

1 Introduction
Most logs an Event Processing Node writes never reach the one tool people use to read them. An Event
Processing Node, an EPN, is one machine of the ALICE online farm. Its job is timeframe reconstruction:
it assembles the detector data of one time slice into events. The reconstruction software is O2, the ALICE
Online-Offline computing system [3]. Log search must not compete with that job.
Logging today is InfoLogger [2]. A client library sends each process’s messages to a local daemon, then to
one central server. That road has three limits: one table for every message, no counts over time, and one
unreplicated retention rule. The process logs on disk and the journal, the log of every system service, never
take it (Figure 1).

We built loggy, a platform that keeps the bulk of the logs on the worker that wrote them and still searches
them as one. It runs on five staging machines, and one farm deployment passed every check.
Section 2 gives the constraints. Section 3 walks an error line, an info line, a dead collector and a quiet
worker through it. Section 4 names the fact behind each choice, Section 5 what the measurements settle,
and Section 6 the limits and what remains.


2 The problem and the constraints
The constraints made the design, so they come first.

2.1 What a node writes
A worker holds five log families, and InfoLogger is one of them (Table 1). DDS, the Dynamic Deployment

The survey of the real machines set two rules. First, the run directory sits on one 14 TB scratch disk, 93 %
full, that every machine mounts over the network. Each collector tails only its own machine’s directory,
or each file lands once per machine. The mount raises no change events, so the tail polls. Second, one O2
process prints raw terminal colour codes into its log, so every anchored pattern fails without a word.
The archive we replay is recorded farm logs, fed back through the collector at a chosen rate. It holds
InfoLogger, DDS and the process tree, and no journal. The parser corpus is 45,596,613 of its lines from 186
programs. DDS is 0.1 % of that corpus, and operations call it the largest family during data-taking. No run
was active during either survey, so no live job log was seen.

2.2 What a worker carries
A real worker carries tens of InfoLogger records a second, not thousands. The InfoLogger archive, six
months of dumps, holds 248,828,513 records from 312 hosts. In the busiest hour the median worker carried
23 records a second, the busiest worker-second 78, and the farm peaked at 9,781. These figures are floors:
the client library cuts off a process at 1,000 messages a minute, so the loudest processes are hidden.
The soak, the project’s measurement campaign of twenty-one rounds, tested one collector at 1,000, 20,000
and 50,000 records a second per worker. Those rates are a safety margin, not an archive figure.

2.3 The constraints
A worker gives the platform four physical cores, eight logical processors, and little memory. A 2019 EPN
has 64 physical cores and 128 logical processors. No bulk crosses the wire: each worker indexes its info-
severity lines on its own disk. Physicists’ staging runs occupy two of the three farm workers and a build
pipeline wipes the third, so hard memory and processor caps apply.

The infra machine’s default ports belong to a second search cluster, so the storage nodes and Alertmanager

2.5 The 2025 reference design
The 2025 reference design [1] set the frame, and loggy kept its shape and changed its rules. We kept
its collector on every node and widened it from two sources to five. Its one cluster of two tiers, three
index families, lifecycle policies and Alertmanager stayed. We changed its tier rule from source to severity,
because a source rule ships one program’s bulk and drops another’s errors. We replaced Grafana with
OpenSearch Dashboards, the store’s own web interface, because detection and alerting come with the
store [5]. We dropped its queue on the numbers in Section 5.3, and the aggregator the queue fed, because
the collectors feed the live lane directly. We dropped Kubernetes for native system services pushed over
SSH.


3 The design
loggy is one cluster, split in two by severity.

3.1 One cluster, two tiers
The worker tier runs on every EPN in the target design. The collector reads the five log sources. The
stamper beside it names every line’s template, the skeleton of the line with every variable token a wildcard,
before indexing. The health sample pushes the collector’s heartbeat up. The local OpenSearch data node
holds one index per worker. The four share the worker’s four-core budget of Section 2.3. Memory is a
384 MB cap on the collector and a 1 GB heap on the local node.
The storage tier is three replicated nodes, each a cluster manager and a data node. An index is split into
shards, and a replica is a copy of a shard on another node. Two of the three nodes must agree, so the tier
survives the loss of one. The three storage nodes are also the control host, the background host and the
shifter host. The control host holds every screen: Dashboards, and Alertmanager, which decides when a
person is told. The poller samples the cluster. The roster lists the collectors that must run. The catalogue
maintenance audits the template counts. The receiver stores every notification Alertmanager sends. Noth-
ing pages a human today. The background host runs the projector, which turns alerts into episodes, and
the trend rollup. The shifter host serves the shifter view, the page the person on shift watches. Staging is

3.3 Walkthrough: an info line stays on its worker
An info line takes the same road as far as the routing rule, then stays on its worker. A placement setting
pins the index there. Under severity routing 96.94 % of the O2 process-tree family stays local and 3.06 %
crosses the wire. The trend rollup, six of the seventeen detectors of Section 3.6, and the shifter view’s line
sampler read the local index. If the worker dies, its raw lines die with it, but the rollup rows, template
counts and detector results survive. One search still finds the line. That search is as slow as the slowest
worker, which is why the live lane exists.

At most 64 chunks sit in memory, about 128 MB. The documented worst case is twice that with a 20 %
margin, roughly 307 MB. The service is throttled at 384 MB and killed at 768 MB.
The disk buffer is a hiccup layer of 256 MB per output: when the store does not answer, chunks spill to disk.
At 20,000 records a second it held 61 seconds before the first drop. At a real worker’s rate it holds hours,
but it is not durability: a lost machine loses its records.
A resend cannot duplicate. The document identifier, node id plus boot id plus a counter, is the record’s id
in the store. The output writes with create, which the store refuses when that id already exists.

3.5 Templates in band
A template widens as the miner sees more lines, and each state is one version, the field a record carries
(Figure 5).
The stamper runs drain3, a template miner [7]. A masker replaces varying tokens with placeholders, then
one tree per family turns a literal that varies between lines into a wildcard. Every record leaves with
a status: matched, new or no template. An empty message, or one over 4,096 characters, is indexed in
full with no template. The stamper holds at most 20,000 templates per worker, 4.7 times what the whole
archive produced. The shipped service evicts the least recently used template at that ceiling. The soak
showed that an evicted template rebuilt later resets its published count to one. The recommended change,
to stop learning and keep counting, is open.
The catalogue is one index with one document per template version the farm has seen, plus the results of
the checks below. Every 300 seconds the stamper publishes one bucket document per family and window:
a total and a count per version. Two audits run hourly. The stamper recounts the previous hour locally.
The catalogue maintenance proves that up to 5,000 hourly buckets sum to their totals. It also proves that
neither shared index holds more records of a template version than the workers stamped. Two hourly rules
read the catalogue. One fires on a template catalogued in the last hour. The other fires on any failed check
in the last two hours.

3.6 Watching the platform
Everything that watches the platform is pushed up, never polled down. Every collector ships a health
sample every 30 seconds. The poller samples the cluster at the same cadence and reads the roster, one
document naming every collector that must run: the fleet. A rostered collector silent for 90 seconds is
absent.
Three lanes read numbers: health samples, and counts, error rates and lags from the log records. A monitor
is a saved rule the cluster runs on a schedule, writing an alert when its condition holds. Threshold monitors
catch cliffs in two tiers: a storage disk above 92 % pages, and above 85 up to 92 % warns. A detector is a
scheduled Random Cut Forest model [8]. It learns a number’s usual shape and grades each window from 0
to 1 by how unusual it is, with no threshold. An entity is one machine, or one log family on one machine:
the thing a rule is about.
Seventeen detectors run, one model per entity. A volume detector reads a silent host as zero records, so
silence is a value it can grade. One forecaster, on disk fill only, trains on a week of hourly points and
predicts a day ahead. Thirty monitors run: 13 every minute and four every ten minutes on thresholds and
detector results. Nine are trend comparisons every ten minutes, two run hourly on templates, two watch
the projector and Alertmanager themselves. No lane reads the log text (Section 6.3).
On the background host the projector reads every alert and detector result every 30 seconds (Figure 6). A
signal is one stored row: which rule or detector fired, on which entity, when. An episode is one trouble on
one thing, with a start and an end. It stores each episode as one incident document, grouping the signals
of one alert name on one entity. An episode closes after consecutive healthy windows: one for a threshold
monitor, three for a trend monitor or detector. A grade above 0.5 opens an episode. One monitor watches
every grade and pages once for the fleet above 0.7.
Alertmanager owns notification semantics only: grouping timers, silences and routing [9]. It forgets an
alert five minutes after the last post, so the projector re-posts every open episode every 30 seconds. It holds

3.7 Walkthrough: a dead collector becomes a notification
A dead collector is silent, so the platform reasons from absence. Within 30 seconds the poller writes the
collector’s row with a missing flag. The collector-down monitor reads those rows every minute and fires at
page severity. The projector opens an episode and posts it to Alertmanager. Alertmanager groups on alert
name and collector, waits 30 seconds and posts the batch to the receiver.
The cadences bound death-to-document at 90 to 270 seconds. The first missing flag arrives 60 to 150 seconds
after the death, because the last sample can already be 30 seconds old. The monitor adds up to 60 seconds,
the projector up to 30 and the page wait another 30. No run has timed it (Section 6.1).

3.8 Walkthrough: an EPN goes quiet, or loud
On the background host a rollup turns raw lines into small rows: one per family and host per ten-minute
slice. It runs every ten minutes, waits 120 seconds for late records, then recomputes the last three finished

slices. Each row holds record, error and fatal counts and the 95th percentile of two lags. A host that logged
in the last 24 hours but not this slice gets a zero-count row. A host silent for longer gets none.
The nine trend monitors read those rows only. Each compares three ten-minute slices, ending ten minutes
before the check, against the previous seven days. A volume rule compares the entity’s share of the fleet,
so a fleet-wide ramp cancels. It fires on twice the baseline share or half of it, in all three slices. It needs
50 records per slice and six baseline slices. The two shipping-lag rules compare the entity’s own lag against
twice its seven-day baseline, floored at 250 ms. A separate monitor pages when a family averaging at
least 50 records a slice over 24 hours writes nothing for 40 minutes. Trend alerts are the warn tier, so
Alertmanager batches per rule name. Which messages changed is the template lane’s question (Section 3.5).

3.9 The surfaces
Everything a person opens sits behind one door on the control host: one address, one account, four surfaces.
Dashboards, the store’s own web interface, holds the maintainer cockpit and Discover, its search screen for
the archive [5]. The shifter view is separate. Its live page keeps what collectors post: every InfoLogger
record, the daemon log and every line above info severity. That page never queries the cluster, so it holds
when the cluster is down. Its Templates page shows the catalogue, counts and open episodes. Two clocks
run. Health samples and the log-counting detectors use the collector’s accept time. Discover uses the time
the line was written. The operator view close to InfoLogger is not built.

Native services, and containers on one machine. Staging runs vendor packages as system services,
because a container runtime on OpenStack is a second runtime. The farm gives the storage tier one shared

The surfaces. Dashboards holds the maintainer cockpit and Discover because it ships the detection and

The stamper in band. The stamper runs beside the collector, so no raw line leaves its machine. It runs
drain3 [7], because no collector filter language can host it. Without ground truth, cost chose the parser:
PIPLUP, another log-template parser, costs 2.56 times drain3 against a gate of plus or minus 25 %. The
masker, which replaces variable parts of a line with placeholders, was 74 to 87 % of the mining cost, so we
rewrote it. Masking fell from 11.93 to 1.82 core-seconds per million lines on InfoLogger, one core-second
being one core busy for one second. On the process tree it fell from 12.24 to 1.73, on DDS from 40.22 to 5.09,
and no template changed. Mining the parser corpus of 45,596,613 lines now costs 8.33 core-seconds per
million. The first version’s 19.91 was measured once and not re-measured, so we quote no ratio between
the two. The stamper’s cost is not measured.

Detection on numbers, not on text. Three lanes read numbers about the logs inside the cluster, and
Random Cut Forest detectors [8] read a silent host as zero records. Deep sequence models score about 0.23
to 0.27 on F1 once the logs drift from the data they learnt on. F1 weighs faults caught against false alarms,
and 1 is perfect. Plain principal component analysis scores about 0.71 on the same logs. Three routes for
text stay open, and none is chosen.

Episodes, and Alertmanager as messenger. The projector turns thirty rows about one dead collector
into one episode. Alerting alone repeats the alert every minute. Alertmanager [9] stores nothing durable,
so the projector re-posts every open episode. Inhibition, where one alert silences another it causes, is built
and ships off. All 22 cause-and-effect links between alerts are unproven.

Kafka between the collector and the store. The soak rejected a queue between the collector and the
store. Decoupling: a worker’s busiest second in six months is 78 records, the farm’s 9,781. One worker’s
stack sustains about 42,000 a second, a margin of 538 times, or 54 times if the archive under-counts ten
times. Durability: only InfoLogger is lost in an outage, and its 256 MB disk buffer covers 17.4 hours of it at
a median worker’s rate. Cost: three brokers on memory the staging machines lack, and the tier rule splits
into two places. Section 5.3 gives the four conditions that reopen it.

Kafka for the live lane. The September 2026 rework agreed a Kafka [11] bus for the live lane only, for
decoupling and not durability, and it is not built. Today a moved shifter view means editing more than 200
collectors, with a bus one consumer. It must be Apache Kafka, because CERN requires fully open source
software. It must be the primary path, because the collector held at release 4.0.1 has no route for a failed
output. Where the brokers run is not decided.

Deployment. The agent decided it. An agent is a program the tool installs and leaves running on every
machine. Ansible [10] has none: it pushes over SSH when a person starts a run, and creates the machines
too. Puppet wants an agent and a certificate on every machine, likely right for the farm and wrong for five
machines one person deploys by hand. Salt and Chef also want an agent, and Terraform builds machines
but does not configure them.

Cockpit metrics beside Telegraf and Mimir. The poller marks absence against a roster. CERN already
runs Telegraf and Mimir for machine metrics, so we concede our cluster and node samples. The roster and
absence logic have no equivalent there, so they stay.

Shared code as one package. Shared Python becomes one versioned package, agreed and not built.
About 31,000 lines of Python run the services, and five modules are byte-identical copies across them.

5 What the measurements say
The numbers settle what the stack costs a worker, its ceiling, and what templating costs. Every figure comes
from a laptop rig. Shapes, knees and rankings transfer to the farm. Absolute rates and core-seconds do not.

5.1 The instrument and its limits
The instrument sums every container’s processor time into core-seconds, one core busy for one second.
Cost is core-seconds per million records.
Two rigs exist, never averaged. The healthy rig tested the collector’s settings at 20,000 records a second.
The re-run rig, the same laptop slowed, offered 5,000. An arm is one setting under test, and a cell is one
arm at one rate.
The noise floor is the spread of three control runs: 1.61 % at 20,000 records a second and 9.48 % at 1,000.
Cost per record falls as rate rises: the collector alone costs 75.32 core-seconds per million at 1,000 a second
and 11.19 at 20,000.
The rig saturated unnoticed after that first pass, so we retracted every later measurement. A 47-cell re-run
left one product change: the flush interval.

5.2 The collector under load
The collector’s memory sat between 133 and 228 MB at every rate up to 53,000 records a second. In a sink
outage with the shipped 256 MB buffer it peaked at 373 MB, under its 384 MB memory line.
The flush interval is how often the collector pushes buffered records out. Table 6, from three alternating
runs, is why 1 second ships. Across eight flush values from 0.125 to 10 seconds, whole-stack cost is a U
with its floor at 1 second, best to worst 17.9 % apart.

The live lane of Section 3.1 is the largest cost any setting adds: 41 to 68 % more collector processor time on

A fifteen-minute sink outage at 20,000 records a second loses 66.7 % of InfoLogger at the 256 MB buffer cap.

merging cuts throughput, so the ceiling is the storage tier’s.
Overload is safe. At 82 % of the 256 MB buffer cap the collector absorbs the shortfall and loses nothing. At
the cap it discards oldest-first, 1.8 % of records, without error, then drains in 263 seconds.

1 GB ships. Core placement between collector and store moved it 2.7 % against a 5.5 % floor. The stack
needs four exclusive cores away from reconstruction. The rig always pinned them, so sharing them is not

5.3 Kafka between the collector and the store, decided on numbers
The soak rejected a queue between the collector and the store on three counts. Decoupling: the stack
sustains 538 times the busiest worker-second and 4.3 times the farm’s peak second of 9,781. If the archive
under-counts by ten times, the margin is 54 times. Durability: the collector’s buffer holds hours at a real
worker’s rate. Cost: three brokers and a second system to run, on memory the machines do not have.
Two rates reopen the question: a sustained worker rate above about 1,000 records a second, or one within
50 times of 42,000. Two needs do too: a second consumer, or an outage longer than the buffer holds. The
live-lane bus is separate.

5.4 The other sources and the real node
The collector had never read three of the five families. Those are the second process-tree format, written
by the data-distribution processes, the InfoLogger daemon log and the system journal. The first fixture
run sent 41.8 % of the corpus to the storage tier with no severity: a parser anchor missed a date the replay
prepends. We fixed the anchor. Severity now comes out of 99.83 % of process-tree lines, against a 99 % gate.
96.94 % of that family stays on the worker.
A read-only census of four farm machines corrected four facts. Four installations run there, on three re-
leases where we assumed two. The storage machine’s release is two majors behind and untested. All four
passed the fixture and restart checks. A later review found the rotation check itself was flaky. Release 5.0.8
loses every byte appended after rotation, in five of five runs, so it is blocked. A worker’s journal is 2,500 to
10,500 entries a day, nothing beside millions of process-tree lines, so the collector reads all of it.
An external review retracted every collector regex cost figure. Two identical configurations differ by up
to 19.3 % in wall clock, so the rig cannot resolve a change below about a fifth. No regex change is worth
making on cost.
We fixed 99 defects, 32 in the instrument, the rest in the collector, the stamper and the catalogue. A retried
bulk write duplicated every record, so the product now writes with create under a stamped document
identifier.

5.5 Templating
Templating is built, 31 to 57 % cheaper per family than its first version, with identical output (Table 7). Costs
are core-seconds per million lines on the rig of Section 5.1.
With its own defaults and one global tree, drain3 mined the parser corpus into 3,822 templates at the
unverified 19.91 core-seconds per million [6, 7].
A recipe is one family’s depth, similarity and separator setting. One per family costs the same as one global
setting, 17.57 against 17.65 weighted core-seconds per million. Contentless infologger templates fell from
44.9 to 10.4 %.
With the recipes and the rewritten masker, one run of each family on the same 45,596,613 lines gave 3,011
templates, a floor. Sixteen runs and eighty InfoLogger partitions gave 4,092. One tree across the three
corpora behind the recipes, 55,963,050 lines, gave 4,221. The count grows with runs sampled, not lines.
New templates arrive at 48.9 per million, about one a second at 20,000 records a second. The tree is capped
at 20,000 templates, 208.1 MB inside the stamper’s 512 MB limit.
Splicing the stamped fields into the record’s own bytes cut the transport step 15 %. The whole collector-
to-stamper hop, of which transport is roughly a quarter, fell 6.5 % on infologger and 4.0 % on the process
tree. Repeated key names are 32 % of that wire, free on a Unix socket, so we rejected protobuf and gzip
unmeasured.

5.7 The detection chain, proven by injection
Seven scenarios each score whether a signal, an incident and a notification followed one thing broken on
purpose. A fault agent on each worker can kill its collector, and the one beside the projector can stop only
the projector. A poison replay floods the indices with marked fake errors. The stopped-projector run passes
only if exactly one stale-projector notification takes the break-glass path of Section 3.6. The scorer checks
presence and order, not time, so the bound in Section 3.7 follows from cadences and is not measured.

6.1 Not measured
Every cost figure in Section 5 comes from a laptop. One micro-benchmark on one farm machine gives the
conversion factor. None has run.
The rig gave the worker’s collector and store four exclusive cores, and the storage tier four more. Sharing
them with reconstruction is not measured.

20,000. That band matches the farm-wide peak of 9,781, so the plan probably read a farm rate as a worker
rate. No run measured a burst.
The whole stack’s cost was measured at 5,000 records a second only. The collector alone was also costed
at 1,000 and 20,000. Round 1’s 53,000 was read against a test output that always accepts, so the collector’s
ceiling with a real store is unknown above 50,000.
The dead-collector chain of Section 3.7 is bounded at 90 to 270 seconds by cadence, and no run timed it.

The injection harness waits 32. Neither was measured.

measured on a worker.

6.2 Limits found in the design
The stale-projector monitor fires only while one projector heartbeat exists in the last 24 hours, so a day
of silence mutes it. The Alertmanager-down monitor reads the projector’s own report, so a dead projector
silences it too.
No inhibition rule is active, because all 22 causal edges between alerts ship unproven.
Nothing authenticates inside the cluster, so one password and a firewall rule are the whole boundary, on
five machines we own.
The live lane carries InfoLogger, the daemon log and the central family only. Process output lines with no
severity went central before the fix of Section 5.4, so the live page now shows fewer.
DDS is the densest family during data-taking and the dearest per line. The recipe runs under-sample it at
43,972 lines, 0.1 % of the weight, and the later runs used 1,236,971 lines.
At its 20,000-template ceiling the stamper evicts a template, a rebuilt one restarts its count at one, and the
change to keep counting is open.

shifter view have run on staging only. Production, the whole farm with at least three storage machines,
two replicas and the bus, is not real.

2. The shared Python becomes one versioned package, agreed.
3. The Telegraf decision is open: whether CERN’s metrics estate takes over the samples.
4. Catalogue maintenance as ISM policies plus monitors is open. ISM, Index State Management, deletes

5. The operator view beside InfoLogger waits for farm access and a live run.
6. The system journal and per-device process logs are absent from the archive.
7. Anomaly detection on the log text stays open [14]: templates, then nearest-neighbour search. Raw-
   message search and templating as the detector lost.
8. Authentication inside the cluster is required before the farm.
9. Source-owner approval for reading the node logs is open.
