\section{What the measurements say}\label{sec:eval}

The numbers settle what the stack costs a worker, its ceiling, and what templating costs. Every figure comes from a laptop rig. Shapes, knees and rankings transfer to the farm. Absolute rates and core-seconds do not.

\subsection{The instrument and its limits}\label{sec:eval-instrument}

The rig runs the whole stack in containers. The instrument sums every container's processor time into core-seconds, one core busy for one second. Cost is core-seconds per million records.

Two rigs exist, never averaged. The healthy rig tested the collector's settings at 20,000 records a second. The re-run rig, the same laptop slowed, offered 5,000. An arm is one setting under test, and a cell is one arm at one rate.

The noise floor is the spread of three control runs: 1.61~\% at 20,000 records a second and 9.48~\% at 1,000. Cost per record falls as rate rises: the collector alone costs 75.32 core-seconds per million at 1,000 a second and 11.19 at 20,000.

The rig saturated unnoticed after that first pass, so we retracted every later measurement. A 47-cell re-run left one product change: the flush interval.

\subsection{The collector under load}\label{sec:eval-collector}

Against a sink that always accepts, the collector's memory sat between 133 and 228~MB on two cores, up to 53,000 records a second. In a sink outage with the shipped 256~MB buffer it peaked at 373~MB, under its 384~MB memory line.

The flush interval is how often the collector pushes buffered records out. Table~\ref{tab:flush} comes from three alternating runs. Across eight flush values from 0.125 to 10 seconds, whole-stack cost is a U with its floor at 1 second, best to worst 17.9~\% apart.

Every threading arm cost more against the 1.61~\% floor: a second collector process 7.4~\%, a thread per source 11.5~\%, and parsing moved into those threads 26.5~\%. One single-threaded loop does 89.3~\% of the collector's processor work, so threads elsewhere cannot help it.

The live lane of Section~\ref{sec:design-tiers} is the largest cost any setting adds: 41 to 68~\% more collector processor time on the healthy rig.

A fifteen-minute sink outage at 20,000 records a second loses 66.7~\% of \textit{InfoLogger} at the buffer cap. That buffer holds 865,674 \textit{InfoLogger} records of 310~bytes, and \textit{InfoLogger} is 60~\% of a worker's mix. At a median worker's 23 records a second \textit{InfoLogger} is 13.8, and the buffer covers 17.4~hours. At the busiest worker-second, 78, it is 46.8 and the buffer covers 5.1~hours.

The stack sustains about 42,000 records a second. It holds 50,000 for two minutes with zero loss. The storage tier runs at 86~\% of its four cores. The decline starts near six million documents, where index merging cuts throughput, so the ceiling is the storage tier's.

Overload is safe. At 82~\% of the buffer cap the collector absorbs the shortfall and loses nothing. At the cap it discards oldest-first, 1.8~\% of records, without error, then drains in 263 seconds.

Two settings make no difference. On the re-run rig a 2~GB heap moved cost 2.1~\% against a 6.7~\% floor, so 1~GB ships. Core placement between collector and store moved it 2.7~\% against a 5.5~\% floor. The rig always pinned four exclusive cores, so sharing them with reconstruction is not measured.

\subsection{Kafka between the collector and the store, decided on numbers}\label{sec:eval-kafka}

The soak rejected a queue between the collector and the store on three counts. Decoupling: the stack sustains 538 times the busiest worker-second and 4.3 times the farm's peak second of 9,781. If the archive under-counts by ten times, the margin is 54 times. Durability: the collector's buffer holds hours at a real worker's rate. Cost: three brokers and a second system to run, on memory the machines do not have.

Two rates reopen the question: a sustained worker rate above about 1,000 records a second, or one within 50 times of 42,000. Two needs do too: a second consumer, or an outage longer than the buffer holds. The live-lane bus is separate.

\subsection{The other sources and the real node}\label{sec:eval-sources}

The collector now reads three log sources it had never read before. They are the second process-tree format, written by the data-distribution processes, the \textit{InfoLogger} daemon log and the system journal. Severity now comes out of 99.83~\% of process-tree lines, against a 99~\% gate, and 96.94~\% of that family stays on the worker. The first fixture run sent 41.8~\% of the corpus to the storage tier with no severity. A parser anchor missed a date the replay prepends.

Four facts in the design now come from a read-only census of four farm machines. Four installations run there, on three releases where we assumed two. The storage machine's release is two majors behind and untested. A worker's journal is small beside millions of process-tree lines, so the collector reads all of it.

The collector passes the fixture and restart checks on the three farm releases and on one newer. Release 5.0.8 is blocked: it loses every byte appended after rotation, in five of five runs. The rotation check now rotates only after a line written before it has reached the output, so the loss shows on every run.

No regex change in the collector is worth making on cost. Two identical configurations differ by up to 19.3~\% in processor time, so the rig cannot resolve a change below about a fifth. For that reason an external review retracted every collector regex cost figure.

The product now writes with create under a stamped document identifier, so a retried bulk write no longer duplicates every record. That was one of 99 defects fixed in the test rounds: 32 in the instrument, the rest in the collector, the stamper and the catalogue.
